# Vibrato

The Plus course in four units, 17 lessons: a steady straight tone first, then an even wave you steer,
then letting it happen, then choosing it. Its plan and measures are in
[plus-courses-build-plan.md](../plus-courses-build-plan.md) (sections 3 and 4); this page keeps its
facts and their sources. The course file is `course/plus/vibrato/course.json` in the courses repo,
written by hand (not generated).

## Facts, and where they're told

Each is its lesson's `fact`, the lesson end's "Did you know?" card, with its source. None is told in another course.

| Lesson | Fact | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| vib-1-hear | In the 1930s, Carl Seashore's studies at the University of Iowa found professional singers used vibrato on about 95 percent of their notes. | Seashore, *Psychology of Music*, McGraw-Hill 1938, ch. 4 "A Musical Ornament, the Vibrato" (archive.org/details/psychologyofmusi030417mbp); McKinney, *The Diagnosis and Correction of Vocal Faults*, 1994, p. 197 |
| vib-1-hear (a round's why) | A natural vibrato is about six a second. | Prame, JASA 96 (1994) 1979-84 |
| vib-2-ladder | Classical singers' vibrato usually runs about 5.5 to 7 a second. | Prame 1994 (ten singers, 5.5 to 6.7 Hz); Sundberg, "Acoustic and psychoacoustic aspects of vocal vibrato", in Dejonckere, Hirano and Sundberg (eds.), *Vibrato*, Singular 1995 |
| vib-2-narrow | On a held note, listeners hear the pitch at the middle of the wave. | Shonle and Horan, "The pitch of vibrato tones", JASA 67 (1980) 246-52; d'Alessandro and Castellengo, JASA 95 (1994) (for short notes the end counts more, so "held") |
| vib-3-ghost | Vibrato comes with regular pulses in the muscles that stretch the vocal folds. | Hsiao, Solomon, Luschei and Titze, J. Voice 8 (1994) 224-29 (cricothyroid most strongly); Titze, Story, Smith and Long, JASA 111 (2002), a model of why |
| vib-4-switch | In 1756 Leopold Mozart warned violinists against trembling on every note "as if they had the palsy" (in the usual English translation). | L. Mozart, *Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule*, 1756, ch. 11 §3 (the German says "the perpetual fever"); tr. Knocker, OUP 1948 |
| vib-4-style | Before about 1900 violinists used vibrato now and then; near-constant vibrato spread in the early 1900s, often credited to Fritz Kreisler. | Robert Philip, *Early Recordings and Musical Style*, CUP 1992; Clive Brown, *Classical and Romantic Performing Practice 1750-1900*, OUP 1999 |

Left out: a "wobble" as a slowed, widened vibrato of older or tired voices. "Wobble" has no agreed
threshold, and the ageing studies disagree (McHenry and Evans, J. Voice 2022; the Netherlands Radio
Choir study, Logopedics Phoniatrics Vocology 2013). The lessons only say a wobble is slow and wide.

## What the 2026-10-29 review changed

- One or two facts a unit, from the list above, each checked against its sources.
- "Your vibrato": a vibrato "can take weeks" to come, not days.
- Lesson 1 says to stop if anything tightens or hurts. The course page has its own note: never force
  a wave or shake it from the jaw or throat.
