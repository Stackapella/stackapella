# Plus courses: outlines, and tips and facts for the UI

Status: draft content (2026-10-28), from Hillel's brainstorm with two outside AIs (Gemini and
Grok), merged and checked here. The list of courses and the rules (Plus, lesson 1 free) are in
[plus-courses-plan.md](plus-courses-plan.md); the competition in
[plus-courses-competition.md](plus-courses-competition.md).

How to use it:
- Each course has its skill in plain words, its units, the free first lesson (and why it hooks),
  what the app scores and what it can't yet (with a self-check), one health note, and **tips and
  facts for the UI**: short lines for lesson intros, loading moments, result screens and the course
  card.
- **Checked for the UI** means the claim is well established. **Check** means it's plausible but
  needs a source before it ships. Claims that didn't hold up were left out.
- No living famous people are named as models (our rule); styles are described instead.
- Every fact still gets a final source check by whoever writes the lesson.

## Build order (where demand meets what we can score)

1. **Perfect your pitch**, **Improvising a harmony**, **Vibrato**, **Gospel and soul runs**,
   **Breath and stamina**: high demand and well scored.
2. Plus our two headline courses, **Counterpoint** and **Vibrato**, and one fun hook:
   **Whistling** (the cleanest thing a pitch tracker can hear).
3. Then the rest of the "ready" courses; courses that need timbre, vowels or percussion recognition
   (belting, diction, accents, character voices, beatboxing, overtone singing) wait for that
   listening to exist.

## Singing skills

### 1. Counterpoint
- **Skill:** singing your own line without being pulled onto someone else's.
- **Units:** a melody over a drone; rounds and canons; a descant above a chorus; partner songs.
- **Free lesson 1:** sing "Row, Row, Row Your Boat" and the app answers in three delayed voices, so
  your solo becomes a four-part round. *Why it hooks:* you hear a choir in the first minute.
- **Scores:** pitch and timing while another line pulls at your ear. **Not yet:** blend and balance.
  *Self-check:* can you still hear the other voices while you sing?
- **Health:** don't sing louder to hear yourself; listen more instead.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - "Sumer Is Icumen In", from around 1260, is the oldest known round in English: four voices in
    canon over two more repeating "Sing cuccu".
  - A round works because one melody fits its own harmony when it starts at different times.
  - A drone underneath makes pitch easier to judge: your ear has a steady floor to tune against.
  - Bach wrote a "crab canon" where a second voice plays the same line backwards, and it still fits.
  - Bach's family sang "quodlibets" at reunions: several unrelated songs at once, for fun.

### 2. Vibrato
- **Skill:** a steady straight tone first, then a relaxed, even wave that you let happen.
- **Units:** the straight "laser" tone; slow pitch pulses; letting the wave speed up on its own; the
  delayed vibrato (straight, then blooming).
- **Free lesson 1:** draw an even zigzag with your voice on the live pitch line, slow like a siren,
  then faster until it shimmers. *Why it hooks:* you see the wave you're making.
- **Scores:** rate (cycles a second), width (cents), evenness and when it starts. **Not yet:**
  whether it comes from ease or from shaking the jaw. *Self-check:* is your jaw still and your neck
  soft?
- **Health:** never force it with your jaw or stomach; vibrato comes when tension leaves.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - A natural vibrato usually pulses about 5 to 6.5 times a second.
  - Your ear hears the centre of the wave as the note, even when it swings well either side.
  - In 1930s studies, Carl Seashore found vibrato in almost all the sung time of professional
    singers.
  - Choirs, early music and barbershop often keep a straight tone so chords tune and ring.

### 3. Range and registers
- **Skill:** low notes to high notes, chest to head voice and falsetto, without a crack in between.
- **Units:** chest voice; head voice and falsetto; mapping your break; building the mix.
- **Free lesson 1:** slide from your lowest comfortable note to your highest light squeak and see
  your full range drawn on screen. *Why it hooks:* everyone wants to know their range.
- **Scores:** your span, and whether a slide stays smooth or jumps. **Not yet:** which register a
  middle note is in. *Self-check:* record a slide and listen for the flip.
- **Health:** don't drag a heavy chest sound up past your break.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Your vocal folds are about the size of a thumbnail: roughly 12 to 17 mm in women, 17 to 25 mm in
    men.
  - Two muscle groups trade control as you go up, a bit like changing gear on a bike.
  - Teenage voices crack because the larynx grows fast and the folds lengthen.
  - At the very top, the "whistle register", only a small part of the folds vibrates.

### 4. Belting, safely
- **Skill:** a strong, bright, speech-like high sound without strain.
- **Units:** the "Hey!" call on pitch; bright twang; shaping vowels for high notes; holding the
  finish.
- **Free lesson 1:** call "Hey!" on three rising notes like hailing a taxi, without pushing air.
- **Scores:** pitch steadiness on high notes and holding the end without going flat. **Not yet:** a
  healthy belt versus a pressed shout. *Self-check:* it should feel like effort in your ribs and
  face, never a scratch in your throat.
- **Health:** stop at any tickle, scratch or squeeze; short sessions.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Loudness in a good belt comes mostly from shaping the mouth and throat, not from more air.
  - Human ears are most sensitive around 2 to 4 kHz, the bright ring belters tune toward.
  - Brighter vowels like "eh" and "ah" usually belt more easily than "oo" up high.
  - *Check:* trained belters can reach about 105 to 110 decibels at a metre.

### 5. Breath and stamina
- **Skill:** low, quiet breaths and metering your air for long phrases and soft starts.
- **Units:** low breathing without lifting the shoulders; the steady hiss; quiet clean starts;
  quick catch breaths.
- **Free lesson 1:** the 20-second note: one breath, one steady note, keep the bar lit.
- **Scores:** note length, pitch droop as air runs out, start and stop timing. **Not yet:**
  shoulders rising or a breathy tone. *Self-check:* watch your shoulders in a mirror.
- **Health:** don't lock your stomach or hold your breath until dizzy.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - A huge breath can make singing harder: the extra pressure can push the first note sharp.
  - Good support slows your ribs from collapsing, rather than squeezing air out.
  - Whispering for long can tire the voice more than singing softly.
  - The Italian word "appoggio", to lean, names this balanced kind of support.
  - *Check:* water you drink doesn't touch your vocal folds; it hydrates them later from inside.

### 6. Diction for singers
- **Skill:** tall, steady vowels with crisp consonants just before the beat.
- **Units:** five pure vowels; taming two-part vowels; consonants on the grid; fast patter.
- **Free lesson 1:** a dramatic movie-trailer line: hold the vowels, snap the final consonants onto
  the beat.
- **Scores:** when notes start and end, showing late vowels. **Not yet:** vowel purity.
  *Self-check:* record it and ask a friend which words they caught.
- **Health:** a clenched jaw from over-pronouncing makes high notes harder.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Singers put consonants just before the beat so the vowel, which carries the note, lands on it.
  - Very high notes make vowels hard to tell apart; sopranos adjust vowels up there on purpose.
  - English has many two-part vowels ("I", "now"); singers hold the first part and add the second
    at the end.
  - Fast "patter songs" can ask for several syllables a second while staying on pitch.

### 7. Sight-singing
- **Skill:** reading a stave and singing it, with do, re, mi.
- **Units:** steps from do to so; jumps within the home chord; rhythm and pitch together; short
  melodies at sight.
- **Free lesson 1:** learn do, re and mi on a three-line stave, then sight-read three mystery tunes
  and guess them.
- **Scores:** every pitch and rhythm against the page. **Not yet:** whether you sang the right
  syllable.
- **Health:** keep the screen at eye level.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Do, re, mi come from an 11th-century hymn; the monk Guido of Arezzo used its line starts as
    note names.
  - Guido's ideas let church singers learn new chants much faster than learning by ear alone.
  - "Ut" was later renamed "do", which is easier to sing.
  - "Movable do" means do is always home, whatever the key.

### 8. Ear training
- **Skill:** hearing a distance between notes or a chord and singing it back.
- **Units:** steps; famous-song leaps; major versus minor; singing the root.
- **Free lesson 1:** hear the first two notes of famous tunes and echo the leap from new starting
  notes.
- **Scores:** the interval, reaction time, and landing on the chord tone.
- **Health:** move the exercise to your comfortable range if needed.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - An octave sounds so "same" because the top note vibrates exactly twice as fast.
  - A perfect fifth is a 3 to 2 ratio of frequencies.
  - True tone deafness is rare, around 1 to 4 percent of people; most people who think they can't
    sing just haven't trained ear and voice together.
  - Your brain can "hear" a bass note that isn't there from the notes above it (the missing
    fundamental).
  - Babies hear differences in unfamiliar musical scales that adults miss.

### 9. Perfect your pitch
- **Skill:** landing dead centre without scooping, and tuning to a drone.
- **Units:** clean entries; tuning to a drone; pitch memory anchors; naming a note cold.
- **Free lesson 1:** hear a note, wait through a few seconds of distraction, sing it back without
  sliding in.
- **Scores:** cents off, the scoop in the first moments, drift over time.
- **Health:** stiffening your neck to stay in tune pushes you sharp; relax.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Many people sing a favourite song from memory close to its original key (Levitin's study).
  - Speakers of tone languages like Mandarin are more likely to have absolute pitch.
  - Loud low notes can sound slightly lower in pitch than they are.
  - True absolute pitch is rare, but anyone can sharpen relative pitch with practice.

## Styles

### 1. Scat singing
- **Skill:** horn-like improvised lines on syllables like "doo-bah" that swing and land on the chord.
- **Units:** the swing pocket; landing on chord tones; call and response with a horn; a 12-bar blues
  solo.
- **Free lesson 1:** trade two-bar echoes with a virtual sax over a walking bass, changing your last
  note each time.
- **Scores:** chord-tone landings and swung timing. **Not yet:** the syllables' style.
- **Health:** keep the jaw loose for fast syllables.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - A popular legend says Louis Armstrong dropped his lyric sheet recording "Heebie Jeebies" in
    1926 and scatted instead; scat existed before, but the record made it famous.
  - Cab Calloway published a dictionary of jazz slang.
  - Brain scans of improvising jazz players show self-monitoring areas quieting down (Limb and
    Braun, 2008).
  - Swing isn't one fixed ratio: players swing wider at slow tempos and straighter when fast.

### 2. Barbershop and tags
- **Skill:** four parts tuned so tightly the chord rings.
- **Units:** straight tone and matched vowels; pure tuning; classic tags; the "post" (one long held
  note).
- **Free lesson 1:** sing the end of a classic tag with three recorded voices and tune your last note
  until the chord locks.
- **Scores:** tuning to the cent, straight tone. **Not yet:** matching vowels.
- **Health:** the tenor part sits high; sing it light, never pushed.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Barbershop singers tune major thirds about 14 cents lower than a piano does, so chords stop
    "beating".
  - When a chord locks, the overtones can sound like an extra voice ringing above the four.
  - In barbershop the second-highest voice, the lead, sings the tune; the tenor floats above it.
  - Barbershop harmony has roots in Black American quartet singing of the late 1800s.
  - The barbershop seventh chord is tuned close to the 4:5:6:7 ratios of natural harmonics.

### 3. Gospel and soul runs
- **Skill:** fast, clean runs and bends that stay separate notes, not a smear.
- **Units:** the minor pentatonic; slow-motion steps; blue-note bends; speeding up.
- **Free lesson 1:** a five-note run at half speed, each note lighting a stair, then faster.
- **Scores:** every note of the run. **Not yet:** warmth and grit.
- **Health:** runs need a light voice; heavy runs tire you fast.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Many runs use the minor pentatonic, a five-note scale found in music all over the world.
  - A "blue note" sits between the piano's keys, often a little below the major third.
  - "Lining out", where a leader sings a line and the congregation answers with slow decorated
    lines, is one root of gospel ornament.
  - Learning a run slowly first is how most singers make it clean.

### 4. Yodeling
- **Skill:** flipping cleanly between chest voice and a light high voice, on purpose.
- **Units:** finding the flip; the vowel switch; leaps in rhythm; fast yodels.
- **Free lesson 1:** slide up until your voice flips on purpose, then lock that flip into a cowboy
  yodel hook.
- **Scores:** the jump on the pitch line (a clean break, not a slide) and speed.
- **Health:** keep it moderate; slamming into chest voice again and again irritates the folds.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Alpine yodeling began as herders' calls across valleys; a written description goes back to 1545.
  - Yodelers switch vowels, open below and narrow above, to help the flip.
  - Forest peoples of Central Africa have their own rich tradition of yodeled polyphony.
  - Jimmie Rodgers's "Blue Yodel" records in the late 1920s brought yodeling into country music.
  - *Check:* the record for the fastest yodel is about 22 tones in one second.

### 5. Folk and early music
- **Skill:** plain, storytelling tone over old scales and drones.
- **Units:** straight storytelling tone; Dorian and Mixolydian; grace notes; unaccompanied ballads.
- **Free lesson 1:** sing "Scarborough Fair" over a low drone, keeping the line steady.
- **Scores:** straight tone, the mode's notes, and drifting off key without accompaniment.
- **Health:** a straight tone should feel like a relaxed sigh, not a held throat.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Cecil Sharp found old ballads still sung in the Appalachian mountains in 1916 to 1918.
  - "Scarborough Fair" is in the Dorian mode: minor with a raised sixth.
  - Shape-note singing gave each note a shape so whole towns could learn to read harmony.
  - Many folk tunes skip a note or two of the scale, so they sit well over one drone.

### 6. Musical theatre
- **Skill:** acting a song: talk-like verses, smooth lines and a big finish.
- **Units:** speech-level verses; legato; patter rhythm; the big finish.
- **Free lesson 1:** a four-line build from conversation to a long ringing final note.
- **Scores:** pitch, fast-lyric rhythm, smooth connection, final-note length. **Not yet:** acting.
- **Health:** pace yourself; don't spend everything in the first verse.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Body microphones only became common on Broadway from the mid-1960s; before that singers filled
    the hall unaided.
  - The "eleven o'clock number" is the big late show-stopper, named for when it used to land.
  - Broadway performers often hide their mics at the hairline, a steady distance from the mouth.

### 7. Pop and country styling
- **Skill:** slides, fall-offs, little cries and breathy starts that make a plain tune sound
  recorded.
- **Units:** scoops and fall-offs; the country cry; breathy versus bright; styling a hook.
- **Free lesson 1:** take a flat melody and add a scoop at the start and a fall-off at the end,
  following curves on screen.
- **Scores:** the shape and timing of slides and cries. **Not yet:** breathiness and twang.
- **Health:** breathy singing dries the voice; keep water nearby.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Crooning began when electric microphones arrived around the late 1920s; Bing Crosby was an
    early star of the quiet, close style.
  - Auto-Tune was invented by Andy Hildebrand, an engineer who had worked on oil exploration using
    sound.
  - The country "cry" is often a small flip near the voice's break.

### 8. A cappella bass
- **Skill:** the group's bass guitar: low notes, bass syllables and a steady groove.
- **Units:** relaxed low notes; "dm", "buh" and "doom"; root and fifth grooves; walking lines.
- **Free lesson 1:** lay down a classic bassline under a three-part group.
- **Scores:** low pitch, staying low, and tight starts and stops on the beat.
- **Health:** never tuck the chin to reach low notes.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Russian church "oktavists" sing an octave below an ordinary bass.
  - Some basses use vocal fry or subharmonics to go even lower.
  - Thurl Ravenscroft, a gospel quartet bass, also sang "You're a Mean One, Mr. Grinch".
  - A low C (about 65 Hz) has a sound wave over five metres long, which is why bass carries through
    walls.

### 9. Singing in another language
- **Skill:** a song's sounds and rhythm first, by ear, then the words.
- **Units:** open Italian and Spanish vowels; French and Portuguese nasal colours; rolled R's and
  light consonants; learning a chorus by ear.
- **Free lesson 1:** learn a public-domain chorus ("O Sole Mio") only by echoing short chunks.
- **Scores:** pitch contour and syllable timing against the guide. **Not yet:** pronunciation.
- **Health:** don't tense the tongue for unfamiliar sounds.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Singers often lose their speaking accent in song, because melody sets pitch and vowel length.
  - Opera singers learn songs in languages they don't speak using the International Phonetic
    Alphabet.
  - Many people who stutter can sing fluently.

## Beyond singing

### 1. Whistling
- **Skill:** clear, in-tune whistled melodies, long notes and even vibrato.
- **Units:** the sweet spot; moving pitch with the tongue; clean leaps; whistle vibrato.
- **Free lesson 1:** tune your whistle to the pitch line and whistle a short classic motif on target.
- **Scores:** pitch, range, length and vibrato, very accurately.
- **Health:** no load on your vocal folds; only your lips get tired at first.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - A whistle is close to a pure tone with few overtones, which makes it easy for the app to hear.
  - Your vocal folds don't work at all when you whistle: the mouth makes the sound.
  - On La Gomera in the Canary Islands, people whistle Spanish (Silbo Gomero), protected by UNESCO.
  - *Check:* stagehands on old theatre crews came from ships and signalled with whistles, so
    whistling backstage was once bad luck.

### 2. Beatboxing
- **Skill:** a drum kit from lips, tongue and breath: kick, hi-hat, snare, then grooves.
- **Units:** the B-T-K kit; "boots and cats"; breathing in on the beat; adding a hummed bass.
- **Free lesson 1:** fire kick, hi-hat and snare in time to hit rhythm blocks on a backing track.
- **Scores:** the timing of each hit and a steady tempo. **Not yet:** which drum sound you made
  (needs percussive sound recognition).
- **Health:** the sound comes from air in the mouth and lips, never scraping the throat.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - MRI scans show beatboxers use mouth movements found in clicks and ejectives of some languages
    (Proctor and others, 2013).
  - Inward sounds let beatboxers breathe without stopping the beat.
  - "Beatbox" comes from early drum machines that people copied with their mouths.

### 3. Vocal percussion for a cappella
- **Skill:** the group's steady drummer, keeping time more than showing off.
- **Units:** locking the tempo; open hats and crashes; genre grooves; fills into the chorus.
- **Free lesson 1:** hold the groove under a four-part chorus while the app checks your timing.
- **Scores:** timing on every beat and whether you rushed or dragged. **Not yet:** drum sounds.
- **Health:** small breaths through the nose so you don't gasp and miss beat one.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Vocal percussion in a cappella groups grew in the 1990s to sound like a studio drum kit.
  - Cupping the mic boosts low frequencies, so a soft lip pop sounds like a big kick drum.

### 4. Humming and lip trills
- **Skill:** gentle hums, buzzes and lip bubbles that warm up the voice fast.
- **Units:** the lip trill; the "ng" siren; resonant humming; trill into a sung line.
- **Free lesson 1:** ride a roller-coaster pitch path on one unbroken lip bubble.
- **Scores:** the pitch path, highest and lowest notes, and dropouts. **Not yet:** whether your lips
  are really bubbling. *Self-check:* if the bubble stops, you held your breath or tightened your jaw.
- **Health:** among the safest exercises in voice care; speech therapists use them.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Lip trills and straw singing send pressure back toward the folds and cushion them.
  - Ingo Titze popularised singing through a straw as a voice warm-up.
  - Humming raised nasal nitric oxide about fifteen times in one study (Weitzberg and Lundberg,
    2002).

### 5. Overtone singing
- **Skill:** a low drone with a high whistling melody floating above it.
- **Units:** a bright drone; vowel sweeps; locking the tongue; two-voice melodies.
- **Free lesson 1:** hold one low note and glide from "oo" to "ee" while the screen shows the hidden
  high notes light up. (Needs a harmonics display.)
- **Scores:** the drone's steadiness. **Not yet:** the high melody (needs a second-peak tracker).
- **Health:** stay gentle; don't copy deep, pressed throat styles without a teacher.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - The high notes are already in your voice; your tongue makes one of them loud.
  - Tuvan and Mongolian throat singing grew from herders imitating wind and water.
  - Stockhausen's "Stimmung" (1968) is a long piece built from vocal overtones.

### 6. Live looping
- **Skill:** recording a groove, a bass and harmonies layer by layer, then singing lead on top.
- **Units:** the first clean loop; the low end; the chord pad; lead and mutes.
- **Free lesson 1:** build a four-part loop in about a minute by singing four guided two-bar layers.
- **Scores:** each layer's pitch and timing, and clean loop edges. **Not yet:** blend.
- **Health:** vary your register across layers so one part of your voice doesn't tire.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - In the early 1960s Terry Riley looped tape between two machines to build up live layers.
  - Pauline Oliveros also pioneered tape-delay looping.
  - Stacking many layers on the same warm vowel gets muddy; brighter, thinner layers stay clear.

### 7. Improvising a harmony
- **Skill:** singing a second part to a tune on the spot.
- **Units:** parallel thirds; a held pedal note; stepping off a clash; above or below the tune.
- **Free lesson 1:** sing a third above "You Are My Sunshine" while a green harmony lane lights up.
- **Scores:** consonant intervals and chord tones without copying the tune.
- **Health:** above a loud lead, go lighter rather than straining to match.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Singing a third above means switching between major and minor thirds to stay in the key.
  - Holding one note (a pedal) while the tune moves is one of the oldest harmony tricks.
  - In bluegrass the high harmony above the lead is part of the "high lonesome" sound.
  - Choir singers' heart rates have been measured syncing up as they sing together (Vickhoff and
    others, 2013).

### 8. Character voices
- **Skill:** voices for stories and games by changing pitch, mouth space and rhythm.
- **Units:** pitch and size; placement (nose, cheeks, chest); tempo and melody; two-character scenes.
- **Free lesson 1:** read three lines as a tiny sprite, a booming giant and a flat robot.
- **Scores:** average pitch, how much it rises and falls, and speed. **Not yet:** the character's
  sound itself.
- **Health:** never make a rough voice by scraping; use space and shape, and rest often.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Mel Blanc, voice of Bugs Bunny, has "That's all, folks" on his headstone.
  - Many cartoon boys are voiced by adult women, whose voices don't change mid-series.
  - Some animals, like red deer, have a lowered larynx that makes them sound bigger.

### 9. Accents for performance
- **Skill:** the rhythm, melody and key sounds of an accent for a role or song.
- **Units:** the accent's speech melody; its mouth posture; key vowels and R's; staying in it.
- **Free lesson 1:** hum the melody of a sentence alongside a native speaker before adding the words.
- **Scores:** the sentence's pitch contour and rhythm. **Not yet:** vowels and consonants.
- **Health:** hold the posture lightly; a stiff jaw aches.
- **Tips and facts (checked):**
  - Babies can tell apart the sounds of every language until around their first birthday.
  - English is "stress-timed"; Spanish and French are closer to "syllable-timed".
  - Whether you say the R in "car" is one of the clearest accent markers in English.
  - Foreign accent syndrome is a real, rare condition after some brain injuries.

## Left out, and why

From the outside drafts, these didn't hold up or broke our rules, so they're not used: claims about
vibrato protecting the folds, a 103-second note record, very low "elephant" notes, a vulture-bone
flute tuned to the pentatonic, the cocktail-party effect in rounds, silent vocal-fold movement while
reading music, a nine-notes-a-second run limit, choirs speeding up by a set amount, Broadway as a
10K run, nasal vowels losing half their volume, and every example naming a living famous person.
