# A cappella arranging for beginners

An a cappella arrangement is a song for voices alone: a tune, and a few parts that harmonise with
it. You don't need years of theory to make a simple one, just a handful of ideas and your ears.

## Start with the melody

Everything is built around the tune. Choose a simple song you know well, with a clear tune and
not too many chords, and put it in a key that suits whoever sings it. If the tune is
uncomfortable, every other part suffers too.

## Put a bass on the roots

Next, work out the chords under the tune. Many simple songs use only three: the home chord, and
the chords built on the fourth and fifth notes of the scale. In the key of C, those are C, F and G.

Give the bass the **root** of each chord: the note the chord is named after. Under a C chord, the
bass sings C. This gives the arrangement a firm floor, and it's the easiest bass part to sing.
Later, you can let the bass step between roots now and then to make it walk.

## Fill in the chord

A simple chord has three notes: the root, the **third** and the **fifth**. With a tune on top and
a bass on the root, the alto and tenor share out what's left.

- **Keep the third.** It's the note that makes a chord sound major or minor, so have it in at
  least one part.
- **Keep the upper parts close.** The alto and tenor usually sit within an octave of the part
  above them.
- **Don't let parts cross.** Alto under the tune, tenor under the alto, bass at the bottom, so
  each line is easy to follow.

## Keep every part singable

An arrangement can look fine on paper and be miserable to sing.

- **Keep it in range.** Roughly: soprano C4 to A5, alto G3 to D5, tenor C3 to A4, bass E2 to E4.
  Aim for the middle of each.
- **Move by step, or hold.** Inner parts that stay on a note or move to the next one up or down
  are easy to learn and blend well. Save big leaps for the tune or the bass.
- **Sing each part yourself.** If you can't sing it without effort, a beginner won't either.

A gentle note on one old rule: when two parts move together exactly a fifth or an octave apart
(**parallel fifths and octaves**), they tend to blur into one. Don't worry much at first, but if a
spot sounds hollow, that's often why.

## Words or "oo"?

Backing parts don't have to sing the words.

- **On "oo" or "ah":** soft and blended, and the tune's words stay clear. A bass on "oo" is a
  lovely, simple start.
- **On the words:** fuller, more like a hymn, with everyone telling the story together.

Many arrangements mix the two, like the alto and tenor on the words and the bass on "oo".

## Try it by ear

You can arrange without writing anything down. Record the tune, hum along a little below it (a
third below is a good start), then add a low part on the roots. If it sounds good, it is good. An
instrument can help you find the chords, but it's voices you're writing for.

## Try it in Stackapella

Simple Gifts is a good model: the soprano sings the tune, the alto and tenor stay close beneath it
on easy repeated notes, and the bass holds the roots on "oo". Learn a part or two to hear how
it's built. Then choose **Make your own**:

- **Sing it:** paste the words and sing the tune line by line to a click. The app writes down the
  notes for you to check.
- **Suggest them:** the app makes alto, tenor and bass to fit your tune, in three styles,
  **Simple**, **Warm** and **Bright**. Hear each and pick one.
- If a chord isn't quite right, choose **Try another chord** there. If the tune and the bass move
  in parallel anywhere, the app names the bars.
- Or choose **Write them** and add the parts yourself in the note editor.
- Save it, then **Record** each part over the others, and hear your arrangement in your own voice.
