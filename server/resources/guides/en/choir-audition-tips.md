# Choir audition tips: how to prepare for a choir audition

Most choir auditions are short, friendly and built from the same few tasks. The director wants to
know where your voice sits, whether you can match a pitch, and whether you can hold a line while
others sing something else. Here's what usually happens, and how to prepare for each part.

## What a choir audition usually asks

- **Your range:** a few scales or arpeggios, to hear your lowest and highest comfortable notes.
- **A short song:** something you've prepared, often unaccompanied or with a piano.
- **Singing back:** a short phrase played or sung to you, which you repeat.
- **Sight-reading:** a few bars of simple music to read at sight, in some choirs.
- **Holding a part:** your line in a harmony while someone sings or plays another, in some choirs.

Check the choir's own audition page: many say exactly which of these they ask for, and some post
the music in advance.

## 1. Know your range and your part

Find your lowest and highest comfortable notes before the day, with the free
[vocal range test](/tools/vocal-range-test). It also shows which part fits: soprano, alto, tenor or
bass. The guide to [finding your voice type](/guides/find-your-voice-type) explains what each one
means. You don't need to choose your part at the audition, but knowing it helps you pick a song.

## 2. Choose a short song that suits you

Pick something you know well, in a comfortable key, about a minute long. A simple folk song or
hymn sung in tune shows more than a hard aria sung with strain. Sing it often in the week before,
standing up, the way you'll sing it on the day.

## 3. Practise singing back what you hear

Singing back a phrase tests your ear and your pitch. Practise with the free
[pitch matching](/tools/pitch-matching) tool: hear a note, hold it in your head, sing it back, a few
minutes a day. The guide to [ear training](/guides/ear-training) has more exercises.

## 4. Sight-read a little every day

If the choir asks for sight-reading, practise it daily rather than the night before. Start with
short, simple melodies; read the rhythm first, then the notes. The guide to
[sight-singing with solfege](/guides/sight-reading-for-singers) takes you step by step. If you don't
read music yet, say so: many choirs take singers who learn by ear.

## 5. Practise holding a harmony part

Holding your line while others sing a different one is what choir singing is. Practise it with a
song's parts: on each song's page in [Stackapella's library](/songs), you can hear your part alone,
then sing it while the other parts play, as each note lights up. Try the alto or tenor of
[Amazing Grace](/songs/amazing-grace). The guide to [holding your part](/guides/hold-your-part) has
the tricks.

## On the day

- **Warm up** gently beforehand, so your voice is ready from the first note.
- **Bring water,** and your music if the choir asked for it.
- **Ask for a starting note** if you need one, or bring your own with a
  [pitch pipe](/tools/pitch-pipe).
- **If you slip, keep going.** Directors listen for how you recover, not for a perfect take.

## Common questions

**Do I need to read music to join a choir?** Not always. Many community choirs take singers who
learn by ear; school and auditioned chamber choirs more often ask for sight-reading.

**Which part should I audition for?** The one where your song sits comfortably. Directors often
place you themselves after hearing your range.

**What if I'm nervous?** Everyone is. Choose a song you know well, breathe before you start, and
remember the director wants you to do well.
