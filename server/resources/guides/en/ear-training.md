# Ear training for singers

A "good ear" isn't something you have or don't have. It's what grows when you listen carefully
and sing back what you hear, a little at a time. A few short habits, done most days, make a real
difference to how quickly you learn parts and how well you hold them.

## Habit 1: match a note

Play or hear a single note, then sing it back on "ah" or a hum.

- Listen first, without singing, and imagine the note in your head.
- Then sing it softly. If you're not sure you're on it, slide gently up and down until it
  settles.
- When you match a note, the two sounds seem to melt together. When you're slightly off, you
  hear a faint wobble or roughness between them.

That feeling of "settling" is what you'll use for everything else, so it's worth getting to know.

## Habit 2: hear intervals

An **interval** is the distance between two notes. Harmony is built from a few common ones, and
learning to hear them makes parts much easier to pick up.

A handy trick is to link each interval with the start of a song you know well:

- **A third:** the first two notes of "When the Saints Go Marching In", on "Oh, when". It's the
  sweet, warm distance that a lot of harmony parts sit at.
- **A fifth:** "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star" jumps a fifth from the first "twinkle" to the
  second. It's open and strong: the same distance as from the bottom note of a simple chord to
  its top note, like C up to G.

Sing the song opening, then sing just the two notes, then try to find the same distance starting
on a different note. Don't worry about naming every interval. Knowing these two well will help
more than knowing ten vaguely.

## Habit 3: sing back phrases

Listen to a short phrase, then sing it straight back. Start with three or four notes, then build
up to a whole line.

- Listen to the whole phrase before you sing. Resist the urge to join in halfway.
- Notice its shape: does it go up, down, or stay put? Where's the highest note?
- If you get it wrong, listen again and try once more, rather than guessing a third time.

This is the heart of learning a part by ear, and it gets quicker with practice.

## Habit 4: sing with a chord

Sing a note while a chord plays underneath, and listen for whether your note fits. Then move to
another note of the chord. You'll start to feel which notes belong and which ones clash. This is
the same sense you use when you hold a harmony part.

## Keep it short and regular

- **Five to ten minutes** most days is plenty. Short sessions keep your attention fresh.
- **Pick one habit** a day if you're busy, and rotate.
- **Use songs you like.** It's easier to listen closely to music you enjoy.
- **Headphones help** you hear small differences clearly.

Instruments are welcome too: the same habits help if you play by ear.

## Try it in Stackapella

Are You Sleeping is a round with short phrases that each come twice, so it's made for singing
back what you hear.

- Pick a part and **Listen** to it a phrase at a time with its guide voice.
- Go **On your own**: you hear a phrase, then sing it back while your guide is off. Your notes
  light up as you hit them, so you know at once if you matched.
- Turn on **Chords underneath** and listen for how your notes settle into the chord.
- Set the note names to do re mi or numbers if you'd like to learn how the notes relate to each
  other, not just how they sound.
- When it's easy, **Record** your part, then record the next entry of the round over it.
