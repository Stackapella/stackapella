# How to blend in a choir: match your section's sound

A choir **blends** when you can't pick out one voice: each section sounds like one singer, and
the sections together sound like one instrument. Blending isn't singing quietly or hiding. It's
listening to the people around you and fitting your sound to theirs.

## Why it's worth the work

A blended section is easier to tune, because singers who match each other can hear when they're
not quite together. It sounds fuller, because the voices add up instead of competing. And it
sounds like what most people love about choirs: many voices, one sound.

## How to blend

Six habits, in the order most singers learn them.

## 1. Listen more than you hear yourself

If all you can hear is your own voice, you're too loud for the section. Sing at a level where you can hear yourself and the singers either side of you. A good test: if you can't hear
your neighbour, sing a little less until you can.

## 2. Match the vowels

Vowels are most of the sound a choir makes, and they're where blend is won or lost. Two singers on
the same note with different "ah" vowels sound like two people. Shape each vowel the way your
section does: listen to the singer next to you and copy the shape of their mouth and the colour
of their sound, brighter or darker, rounder or more open.

## 3. Match the volume of your section

Every section has a level the director asks for. Sing inside it. One voice above the rest pokes
out of the sound even when it's singing well, and the rest of the section can't fix that by
singing louder.

## 4. Breathe, start and stop together

Blend is also timing. Breathe where your section breathes (mark the breaths in your copy), start
on the director's gesture, and put final consonants in the same place: a "t" or "s" from twenty
singers at twenty different moments sounds like a hiss. Agree where each one goes, and place it
together.

## 5. Keep your top notes light

High notes are where blend usually breaks: one singer pushes, and suddenly the section has a
soloist. Near the top of your range, use less weight and more of your head voice. Lighter carries
more than you think in a choir.

## 6. Sing in tune with the chord

Two voices slightly apart in pitch beat against each other, and no amount of vowel matching hides
it. Practise holding your note steady against a fixed one: the free [drone](/tools/drone) holds
the key note while you sing, and the [tuner](/tools/tuner) shows where your pitch sits. In a chord, your note may sit a little off the piano's: the [just intonation tuner](/tools/just-intonation-tuner) shows where it rings. The guide
to [singing in tune](/guides/singing-in-tune) goes further.

## Blend in a small group

In a quartet or an a cappella group with one voice on a part, blend works the same way, but you
are the whole section. Balance matters even more: the melody usually leads, and the other parts
sit just under it so the chord sounds full without covering the tune.

Blending starts with knowing your part so well that you can listen to everyone else. On each
