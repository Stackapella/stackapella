# How to hear harmony in a song: pick out each part by ear

When you listen to a choir or an a cappella group, your ear goes straight to the tune. The harmony
is there underneath, but on your own it's hard to pick out: you can't ask the other singers to stop.
Hearing it is a skill, not a gift, and it comes quickly once you can hear each part alone and then
put them back together.

## 1. Start with the tune

The tune (the melody) is the part you already know, usually the highest. Sing it quietly in your
head as the song plays. Everything else you hear is harmony: other notes, sung at the same time,
that fit with it.

## 2. Find the bass next

After the tune, the bass is the easiest part to hear. It's the lowest voice, it moves less often,
and it lands on the note each chord is built on. Listen to the bottom of the sound and hum along
with it. Once you can follow the tune and the bass, you can hear the shape of every chord between
them.

## 3. Then the parts in between

The middle parts are the hardest to hear, because they sit inside the sound. In a choir they're the
alto, just under the tune, and the tenor, just above the bass. Listen for a line that moves when the
tune holds still, or that stays on one note while the tune moves.

## 4. Hear each part on its own, then together

This is the quickest way to train your ear. On each song's page in
[Stackapella's library](/songs), you can hear one part on its own, then all four together, and
switch between them as the song plays. Try it with [Amazing Grace](/songs/amazing-grace): play the
alto alone, then all four parts, and listen for the alto inside the chord. After a few tries you'll
hear it without switching.

## 5. Sing along with one part

Hearing a part is easier once you've sung it. Choose one part and sing along with it quietly, on
"oo", while the others play. Your voice fixes the line in your ear, and next time you listen you'll
pick it out at once. The guide to [singing harmony](/guides/how-to-sing-harmony) goes further.

## 6. Choose songs with clear voices

Start with songs where each voice is easy to hear: a cappella groups, [hymns](/songs/hymns) and
choir music, with few instruments. Four voices are plenty; music with many parts or busy backing is
harder until your ear is used to it.

## What to listen for

- **Above or below?** Is the harmony above the tune or below it? In a choir, both.
- **Together or apart?** Do the parts move together, word by word, or does one hold while another
  moves?
- **Close or wide?** Are the parts close to the tune, a few notes away, or far apart?

The guides to [what harmony is](/guides/what-is-harmony) and [SATB](/guides/satb-explained) explain
the parts and how chords work.

## Common questions

**Why can't I hear the harmony?** Your ear follows the tune first; that's normal. Hear one part on
its own, then listen for it in the whole, and it gets easier quickly.

**Which part is easiest to hear first?** After the tune, the bass: it's lowest and moves least.

**Is hearing harmony a talent?** No. It's a skill, and a few minutes of listening most days builds
it. The guide to [ear training](/guides/ear-training) has simple daily habits.
