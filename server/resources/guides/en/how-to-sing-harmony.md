# How to sing harmony

Harmony is two or more notes sung at once that fit together. The tune is the part everyone knows; a
harmony part sits above or below it and moves with it, sometimes in step, sometimes holding still while
the tune moves. Singing harmony isn't a gift some people have and others don't. It's a skill, and it
comes in a clear order: hear the part, sing it alone, then hold it while something else plays.

## What makes notes fit

Some pairs of notes blend, and some rub. You don't need theory to sing harmony, but knowing which is
which helps you hear what you're aiming for.

- **Thirds and sixths blend.** They sound warm and full, and they're the backbone of most harmony
  parts. A part a third below the tune is one of the easiest harmonies there is.
- **Fifths, fourths and octaves sound open.** Strong and plain, like a frame around the tune.
- **Seconds and sevenths rub.** They're not wrong: they add tension, and usually move on to a note that
  blends.

Hear them for yourself with the free [interval ear training](/tools/interval-ear-training) and
[chord ear training](/tools/chord-ear-training) tools.

## 1. Find the third

Start with one note held under you: a drone. Hum its note until your voice blends with it. Then find
the third: sing do, re, mi slowly, then do and mi without the re in between. Hold mi over the drone
and listen for the moment it settles. Repeat until you can find the third without thinking. That
interval is at the heart of most harmony. The free [drone tool](/tools/drone) plays a drone in any
key.

## 2. Sing a round

A round is the friendliest first harmony, because every voice sings the same tune. One voice starts,
the next comes in a phrase later, and the harmony happens by itself where the lines overlap. All you
have to do is keep your own place.

Try Are You Sleeping (Frère Jacques) or Row, Row, Row Your Boat with a friend, or with a recording of
yourself. When you get lost, don't stop: listen for where you are, and come back in at the start of
the next phrase.

## 3. Two parts in thirds

Next, a tune with a part that sits mostly a third below it, like the alto of Michael, Row the Boat
Ashore here. It holds one steady note while the tune moves above it and meets the tune a third below
on the long notes, so you can feel your part fitting the tune instead of fighting it. This is where most people first hear themselves *making* a harmony rather than just surviving one.

## 4. Learn your part before you hear the tune

From here, every new harmony part follows the same order.

1. **Hear your part alone.** Listen until you could hum it. Most people find a harmony part hard only
   because they haven't heard it enough times by itself.
2. **Sing it with only your part playing.** Quietly, a phrase at a time, learning its shape: where it
   climbs, where it holds, where it moves with the tune and where it doesn't.
3. **Add the tune, softly.** This is the moment the tune pulls at you, and that's normal. If you drift
   onto it, stop, listen to your part again, and come back in.
4. **Turn your part down.** As it settles, make your guide quieter and the others louder, until you're
   holding it with the tune alone.

If you keep sliding onto the tune, there's a whole guide on that: [how to sing harmony when you can only
find the melody](/guides/hold-your-part).

## 5. Low and inner voices

Once thirds feel easy, try the parts that don't follow the tune.

- **The bass** moves in bigger steps, often landing on the key note at the big endings. It's the floor
  the chord stands on: steady and unhurried. Only take the bass if its notes sit in your voice. Never
  press down for low notes: let them be quiet, or sing them an octave up.
- **The inner parts** (alto and tenor) often hold one note while the tune moves around them, and then
  shift by a single step. Holding still while everything else moves is its own skill, and a satisfying
  one.

A part that holds a note while the tune moves is great practice for keeping your line: there's nothing
to follow but yourself.

## 6. Tune it so it rings

Singing the right notes is half of harmony; tuning them is the other half. When voices sing together
without an instrument, the chord sounds best when each note fits the others exactly, and that's not
always where a piano puts it. A major third, for instance, rings when it's sung a little lower than a
piano's, about 14 cents.

You don't tune by numbers. Listen for the blend: when your note fits, the chord goes still and seems to
grow; when it doesn't, you hear a slight roughness. Singing a little more quietly makes the blend easier
to hear.

## 7. Stack your own voices

You can learn all of this alone. Record your part, then learn the next part and sing it over your own
recording. Add the parts that suit your voice, one at a time, and the harmony grows around you. Hearing
yourself in a chord is one of the fastest ways to learn what fitting sounds like.

## Practice habits that help

- **Start slow, with songs that move slowly.** Slow songs give you time to hear each chord.
- **Use headphones, not too loud.** You'll hear the other parts clearly and still hear your own voice,
  so you won't push.
- **Little and often.** Ten minutes most days does more than an hour once a week.
- **Stay in your easy range.** A part that sits too high or too low is harder to tune. Move it down or
  up until it's comfortable.
