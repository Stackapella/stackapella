# How to start an a cappella group: from four friends to a first gig

An a cappella group is a handful of singers and no instruments: the voices are the band. You don't
need auditions, a big room or a director to start one. You need a few people who want to sing
together and somewhere to meet.

## 1. Find your first singers

Start small. Four singers who can each hold a part is enough for a group, and more people join once
there's a group to join. Ask friends, your choir, a school or college notice board, a local music
shop or a community page. Say what you're planning: the style, how often you'll meet, and that
you're starting small.

Aim for a spread of voices: someone who can sing low for the bass, someone comfortable on the
melody, and voices for the parts between. The free [vocal range test](/tools/vocal-range-test)
helps each singer find where they sit, and the guide to [choir voicings](/guides/choir-voicings)
shows which mixes of voices work.

## 2. Choose a style

Agree on what kind of group you are before you choose songs:

- **Barbershop:** four parts, close harmony, ringing chords.
- **Folk and traditional:** songs everyone half knows, often public domain.
- **Pop and contemporary:** a lead singer, backing vocals, a bass line and vocal percussion.
- **Mixed:** plenty of groups sing a bit of everything.

## 3. Pick your first songs

For your first songs, choose short ones in simple harmony, so the group has something that works
in its first few weeks. Use arrangements written for your number and mix of voices. Public-domain songs are free to sing, arrange and record, though a modern arrangement of one can still be in copyright. For anything newer, buy published arrangements, and get permission or a licence before you arrange or record it. When you're ready, the guide to
[arranging a cappella](/guides/arranging-a-cappella) shows how to write your own.

## 4. Learn the parts before rehearsal

Rehearsal time is for singing together, not for learning notes. Ask each singer to learn their
part at home first, so the group can spend its time on blend, tuning and the shape of the song.

## 5. Run short, regular rehearsals

Meet at the same time every week, for an hour or two. A simple plan works:

- **Warm up together,** ten minutes, so voices and ears are ready.
- **Work one song in detail:** a section at a time, chord by chord where it's hard.
- **Sing something you already know,** all the way through, so every rehearsal ends with music.

Choose someone to start the songs and give the starting notes (the free
[pitch pipe](/tools/pitch-pipe) does this), and someone to keep the list of songs and who sings
what. The guides to [blending](/guides/how-to-blend-in-a-choir) and
[running a sectional](/guides/how-to-run-a-sectional) help as the group grows.

## 6. Add vocal percussion if you want it

Pop and contemporary groups often have a singer on vocal percussion, the group's drum kit. It's
optional, and it's a skill of its own: the guide to [vocal percussion](/guides/vocal-percussion)
starts from the three basic sounds.

## 7. Sing for people early

Don't wait until everything is perfect. Sing for friends, at a community event, an open mic or a
local festival. A date to work towards keeps rehearsals focused, and an audience teaches a group
more than another month of practice.
