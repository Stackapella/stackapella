# How to learn your choir part fast, at home

There's never enough rehearsal time to learn every part, so the singers who've practised at home end up
leading their section. The good news: you don't need hours, and you don't need to read music. You need
a plan, and the right order to do things in. Here it is.

## Before you start: map the song

Five minutes spent looking before you sing saves an hour later.

- **Find the repeats.** Most songs repeat: verses with the same tune, a chorus that comes back, an
  ending that echoes the start. Learn each repeated part once, and you've learned it everywhere.
- **Find where your part copies the tune.** In many arrangements a part sings the melody for a while,
  or moves with it note for note. Those bars are almost free.
- **Mark the hard bars.** A jump, a note that clashes on purpose, a place where your part moves when
  the others hold. There are usually only a few. You'll give them extra time later.
- **Mark your breaths.** Decide where you'll breathe, so you never break a phrase or run out at the end
  of one. If a note sits higher or lower than is comfortable, sing it lightly, never louder, and ask
  your director about it.

You can learn a part entirely by ear, the way singers learned songs long before printed parts: hear it,
copy it, check yourself against it.

## 1. Hear your part on its own

Play your part alone and follow the words. Listen two or three times before you sing a note. You're
learning its shape: where it climbs, where it holds, where it jumps.

## 2. Learn it a phrase at a time

Sing along with your part, quietly, one phrase at a time. When the first phrase is secure, add the
second and sing the two together; then add the third. Building it up this way means every phrase gets
sung many times, and the joins between phrases, where singers usually get lost, get practised too.

## 3. Slow the hard bars, at the same pitch

Loop just the hard bars and slow them down, keeping the pitch the same, until they're easy. Then bring
the speed back up a step at a time. A bar you can sing slowly and cleanly will come at speed; a bar you
always rush never does.

Find the notes that help you: the long held note, the first note of each phrase, a note that matches
the tune. These are your anchors. If you wobble in between, you know a safe place is coming.

## 4. Sing it with everyone

Now play all the parts, with yours a little louder than the rest. Each time through, make your part
softer, until you're carrying it yourself. This is the step that makes rehearsal feel easy: the other
parts stop pulling you away. If they still do, there's a guide on exactly that: [how to sing harmony
when you can only find the melody](/guides/hold-your-part).

## 5. Sing it with your part switched off

Turn your part off and let the others carry on. Sing your line into the gap. If you get lost, don't
stop: come back in at the start of the next phrase, as you would in rehearsal.

## 6. Record yourself

Sing it through and listen back. What you hear on the recording is closer to what your section hears
than what you hear inside your head while you sing. Listen for the hard bars and for the ends of
phrases, where notes tend to sag.

## How to fit it in

**Little and often** works better than one long session. Short practice spread over several days
sticks better than the same time crammed into one evening, so if you can, start early.

**A ten-minute routine:**

- **One minute: warm up.** Hum, then slide gently up and down on "oo".
- **One minute: listen.** Your part alone, following the words.
- **Three minutes: phrases.** The next phrase or two, added to the ones you know.
- **Three minutes: with everyone.** All parts playing, yours softer each time.
- **Two minutes: on your own.** Your part switched off, or record yourself and listen back.

**If you only have a few days:**

- **The first day:** map the song, listen, and learn the first section phrase by phrase.
- **The middle days:** the rest of the song, then the hard bars, slowly.
- **The last day:** two or three run-throughs with everyone, then with your part off.

**The night before:** sing it once through with everyone, at full speed, without stopping. Then rest
your voice: one good run-through beats ten tired ones. Sing it once more the next morning, lightly,
after a gentle hum, and you'll know what's stuck.

## Learning the words

If the words are in another language, or there are a lot of them, learn them separately. Speak them in
the song's rhythm before you sing them, so your mouth knows where it's going while your ear handles the
notes. Then put the two together.

## Common traps

- **Learning only from the full recording.** Hearing everyone at once, your part disappears. Start with
  it alone.
- **Always starting from the top.** The first verse ends up polished and the ending stays shaky. Start
  some sessions from the end.
- **Singing everything loud.** An easy, medium-soft voice (not a whisper) is easier to tune and less
  tiring. Save your rehearsal volume for the last run-through.
- **Practising mistakes.** If a bar keeps going wrong, stop singing it fast. Slow it down until it's
  right, then speed it up.

## Try it in Stackapella

**Learn a part** follows these steps:

- **Listen** to your part with its guide voice, a phrase at a time.
- **Sing along** with the others playing, slowing any phrase to 85% or 70% at the same pitch.
- Then go **On your own** while the others keep going, and watch your notes light up as you hit them.

In Sing, you can loop a hard phrase. **Build up to speed** starts it at 70% and goes up 5% each round
you sing cleanly, until it's at full speed. Try it on a carol, a [hymn](/songs/hymns) or a
[spiritual](/songs/spirituals) your choir might sing.
