# How to sight-sing: reading music with solfege

Sight-singing, or sight-reading for singers, means looking at a line of music you've never heard and singing it. It sounds like a gift
some people are born with, but it's a skill built from a few small ones: knowing where home is, seeing
how far each note is from the last, and keeping the beat. A few minutes a day, with lines that are a little too easy, is how most singers get there.

This guide uses do, re, mi, the sol-fa names, called **solfege** in many places, because they name each
note by its place in the key. Do to
mi looks and sounds the same in any key, so what you learn once works everywhere.

## Find do: the home note

Every line starts from home. In do re mi, home is **do**, the first note of the key's scale, and the
key signature at the start of each line tells you where it sits.

- **No sharps or flats:** do is C.
- **Sharps:** the last sharp, the one furthest to the right, sits on **ti**. Do is the next line or space up.
- **Flats:** the last flat sits on **fa**. With two or more flats, there's a quicker way: do sits on the
  next-to-last flat.

Before you sing a note, find do on the stave and hum it, or play it. Every other note is counted from
there.

## Moveable do or fixed do?

In this guide do moves with the key: it's always the home note, so do to mi is the same distance in
every key. That's **moveable do**, the usual way in English-speaking countries. In much of Europe and
Latin America, singers learn **fixed do**, where do is always C, re is always D, and so on, whatever the
key. Both work. Moveable do names each note by its place in the key, so a tune is sung on the same
syllables in any key.

## Read steps and skips

The stave is a ladder. Each line and each space is one note, and higher on the stave is higher in pitch.

- **A step** goes from a line to the next space, or a space to the next line: do to re, re to mi. Most
  tunes move mostly by step.
- **A skip** jumps one line or space: line to line, or space to space. Do, mi and so are each a skip
  apart, the notes of the home chord. Learn to sing them cold, and every leap gets easier.
- **So below do** is a common start: many tunes begin on the so a fourth below do and leap up home.

Ode to Joy is a good first read: its tune moves almost entirely by step. In sol-fa it starts mi, mi, fa,
so, so, fa, mi, re, do. Read it, then check yourself against
[its melody part](/songs/ode-to-joy/soprano).

Before you sing, look at the shape: where does the line go up, where does it turn, where is the highest
note? Then hear the first note in your head before you sing it. If you slip, don't stop: go on to the next
note. In a choir, the music won't wait for you.

## Count the rhythm

The shape of each note says how long it lasts.

- **Crotchet** (a quarter note): filled, with a stem. One beat.
- **Minim** (a half note): hollow, with a stem. Two beats.
- **Semibreve** (a whole note): hollow, no stem. Four beats.
- **Quaver** (an eighth note): filled, with a flag or joined to its neighbour by a beam. Half a beat, so two
  fit in one beat.

- **A dot** makes a note half as long again: a dotted minim is three beats, a whole bar of a waltz.
- **A tie** joins two of the same note: sing it once and hold through.
- **A rest** is silence that still counts. Count it, don't skip it.

Count a bar in before you start, out loud or in your head, and keep the beat going through the whole line,
even through a mistake. A metronome helps at first: slow enough that you can read every note on time.

## The two half steps, and minor

Most steps on the stave are whole steps. Two of them are only half as far, though they look exactly the
same: **mi to fa** and **ti to do**. Sing those close together. Ti in particular leans up into do.

The same notes can make a minor key. Find do from the signature as usual, then **la**, a skip below it.
If the line starts and ends on la, it's in minor: la is home now, and the home chord is la, do, mi. The
signature doesn't change, so you read the notes exactly as before.

## Read your part in a choir

A choir part rarely has the tune. Altos often hold one note while the melody moves above them; tenors
and basses move in their own shapes.

- **Read your line, not the tune.** Keep your eyes on your own stave, even when the melody sounds more
  interesting.
- **Look at the shape first.** Long held notes, then where it climbs or falls.
- **Lost your place?** Find the first note of the next bar and come back in there.
- **Know your clef.** In a choral score with a stave for each part, the tenor is usually written in the treble
  clef, an octave higher than it sounds; in a hymn book the tenor shares the bass-clef stave with the bass.

The first time, read your part on its own. Then read it while the other parts sing around you: that's
the real test, and the one that makes rehearsals easier.

## Hand signs

Many choirs and classrooms add a hand sign to each syllable, so you can feel each note's place as
well as see it. The signs come from John Curwen's method, as Kodály teachers adapted them. Your
hand rises as the notes go up.

- **Do:** a closed fist.
- **Re:** a flat hand, slanting upward.
- **Mi:** a flat hand, palm down, level.
- **Fa:** a fist with the thumb pointing down.
- **So:** a flat hand, palm facing you, level.
- **La:** a relaxed hand, hanging down from the wrist.
- **Ti:** a fist with the first finger pointing up and away.

## A daily habit

- **Five to ten minutes a day** beats an hour once a week.
- **Read something new every day,** a little easier than you can manage. Reading is about the new; the
  old you already know by ear.
- **Find do, look at the shape, count in, then sing straight through.** Same order, every time.
- **Medium volume, in the middle of your voice.** Sight-reading is for the eyes and ears, not for range.

## Where do re mi came from

Around the year 1030, the monk Guido of Arezzo taught singers to read with the first syllables of a hymn
whose lines each started a note higher: ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la. Ut later became do, and a seventh syllable, si, was added; English teachers later changed it to ti. In
the 1800s Sarah Glover and John Curwen turned the syllables into a method for teaching whole classes to
read, and in the 1900s Zoltán Kodály built Hungarian school music on it. Singers have been learning to
read this way for nearly a thousand years.
