# How to sing harmony with yourself

Harmony usually needs other people. But you can sing every part of a song yourself: learn one part,
record it, then sing the next part while you hear the ones you've already recorded. Stack them, and
your own voice becomes a whole chord. It's how one-person a cappella videos are made, and you don't
need a recording studio or music software to do it.

## 1. Choose a song with parts that fit your voice

Start with a short song in four-part harmony that you already half know, so the tune is easy and
your attention goes to the harmony. Hymns, rounds and carols work well; [Amazing Grace](/songs/amazing-grace)
is a good first one.

Check where each part sits. A part far above or below your comfortable range will strain, so find
your range first with the free [vocal range test](/tools/vocal-range-test). Most singers can cover
two or three of the four parts comfortably; for the rest, sing them an octave up or down, as long
as they still sound right against the others.

## 2. Record the tune first

Record the melody first. It's the part you know, and every other part is written to fit around it,
so it becomes your guide for the rest. Sing it steadily and in time: everything else is built on
this take.

## 3. Then the bass, then the parts in between

Next, record the lowest part. The bass holds the chords up, and once it's there you can hear the
shape of each chord. Then record the parts in between, alto and tenor in a choir arrangement, one at
a time, each against everything you've recorded so far.

## 4. Learn each part by ear before you record it

Harmony is hard to hold the first time, because your ear wants to follow the tune. Learn each part
on its own before you record it: listen to it alone, sing along with it, then sing it with the
other parts playing. The guide to [holding your part](/guides/hold-your-part) has more on keeping
your line when the tune pulls you away.

## 5. Wear headphones, and keep every take in time

Record with headphones on, so the microphone only hears your voice and not the parts playing back.
Every part has to start and end with the others: a word that comes in late or a note held too long
shows at once when the parts are stacked. Count in, breathe where the others breathe, and take a
part again if one phrase drifts. The guide to
[recording vocal harmonies at home](/guides/record-vocal-harmonies-at-home) has the setup in detail:
the mic, the room, the timing check and the mix.

## 6. Listen, then share it

Play the whole song back and listen for the parts that stand out: one too loud, one late, a note
out of tune. Fix those with a new take of that part, not by starting again. Then share it.

## Do it in Stackapella

[Stackapella](/how-it-works) is built for exactly this. Pick a song from the library and each part
has a guide voice to learn it from: listen to it alone, sing along, then sing it on your own while
your notes light up. Then record each part over the others, exactly in time: while you record, you
hear your earlier takes play with you. Mix it, export it as a WAV, or share a link. It's free, in
your browser, with nothing to install.

## Common questions

**Do I need to read music?** No. You can learn every part by ear, from a guide voice singing it.

**How many parts can I sing?** As many as the song has. Most beginners start with two (the tune and
one harmony), then add a third and a fourth.

**What if a part is out of my range?** Sing it an octave up or down, or choose a song in a key that
suits you. Men often sing the alto part an octave down, and women the tenor an octave up.

**Why does my recording sound out of time?** A take that starts late or holds a note too long
stands out once the parts are stacked. Wear headphones, count in, and record that part again.
